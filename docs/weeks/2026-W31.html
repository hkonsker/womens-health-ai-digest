Clinical AI Pathology Foundation Model 8

End-To-End Multimodal Pathology Foundation Model with Clinical Dialogue.

Nat Med · Vorontsov E et al. · Jul 31, 2026

PRISM2 is a multimodal pathology foundation model trained on 2.3 million whole-slide images and 14 million question-answer pairs derived from pathology reports, and it matches or exceeds the balanced accuracy of clinical-grade cancer detection products in prostate, breast, and breast lymph node tissue (P<0.05). This is a large-scale, benchmarked advance in computational pathology showing that language-supervised pretraining can produce generalizable diagnostic representations, though real-world deployment validation is still needed.

Explain this simply

Researchers built a giant AI system that reads pathology slides (tissue samples viewed under a microscope) after training it on 2.3 million slide images and 14 million question-and-answer pairs taken from real pathology reports. In head-to-head tests, it matched or beat existing commercial cancer-detection tools for prostate and breast cancer, which is a meaningful step toward AI that understands pathology the way a trained doctor does, though it still needs testing in real clinics.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Fetal Anomaly Screening AI 8

Multicenter Evaluation of an Artificial Intelligence System for Automatic Recognition of Fetal Ultrasound Findings Suggestive of Congenital Malformations.

Obstet Gynecol · Morisset C et al. · Aug 1, 2026

This multicenter retrospective evaluation tested an artificial intelligence (AI) system for detecting eight abnormal fetal ultrasound findings suggestive of congenital malformations across 6,452 images from 1,115 examinations at 75 international sites, achieving mean sensitivity of 93.2% and specificity of 90.8% with consistent performance across manufacturers, regions, and demographics. The large, geographically diverse multicenter dataset and rigorous ground-truth methodology make this one of the more generalizable evaluations of AI in fetal anomaly screening, though it remains a retrospective still-image analysis rather than a prospective real-time clinical trial.

Explain this simply

An AI tool was tested on thousands of ultrasound images from over a thousand pregnancies across 75 hospitals worldwide to see how well it could spot signs of birth defects in the fetal head, chest, and abdomen. The AI correctly identified problems about 93% of the time and correctly ruled them out about 91% of the time, and it worked consistently across different countries, equipment, and patient groups, which is a strong sign the tool could generalize well in real practice.

Clinical AI Model Calibration 7

Tipping the Balance: Impact of Class Imbalance Correction on the Performance of Clinical Risk Prediction Models.

J Am Med Inform Assoc · Andersen AK et al. · Jul 30, 2026

Across ten clinical datasets covering over 600,000 patients and multiple machine-learning model families, class-imbalance correction methods like synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) did not improve area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) or precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) but consistently worsened calibration, as measured by Brier score increases of 0.029 to 0.080. This is a methodologically rigorous, multi-dataset demonstration that a widely used technique for handling rare outcomes can distort predicted probabilities without improving discrimination, which matters directly for anyone building or evaluating clinical risk models.

Explain this simply

Scientists tested a common trick used to help AI models better detect rare medical outcomes, by artificially balancing the data, across ten different real-world medical datasets covering more than 600,000 patients. They found this trick did not make the models better at telling apart people who will versus won't have the outcome, but it did make the model's risk estimates less trustworthy, meaning a predicted 20% risk might not really mean a 20% chance in reality.

Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)

AI in OB/GYN and REI Postpartum Hemorrhage Prediction 7

Risk Stratification for Postpartum Hemorrhage in Vaginal Delivery: A Nationwide Cohort Study of Predelivery and Intrapartum Risk Assessment.

J Obstet Gynaecol Res · Akazawa M et al. · Aug 1, 2026

This nationwide retrospective cohort study of 1,224,990 vaginal deliveries in Japan developed machine learning models for postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) risk stratification, using 2014-2022 data for training and reserving 2023 for temporal external validation, where adding intrapartum data improved sensitivity from 0.537 to 0.596 and discrimination (area under the curve, AUC) from 0.676 to 0.703 at a fixed alert-rate threshold. The huge sample size, genuine temporal external validation, and clinically interpretable metrics (cases detected per 1000 deliveries) make this one of the more rigorous obstetric AI risk-prediction studies this cycle, though the absolute discrimination gains remain modest.

Explain this simply

Researchers built AI models using medical records from over 1.2 million vaginal births in Japan to predict which women are at risk of dangerous bleeding after delivery, testing the models on a full year of more recent patients they had not used to build the model. Adding information collected during labor itself let the model catch a few more at-risk cases without needing to flag many more women overall, which is a meaningful but modest improvement worth watching for future clinical adoption.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Preterm Birth Prediction 7

Leveraging Administrative Health Records and Machine Learning for Population-Level Prediction of Preterm Birth.

Am J Obstet Gynecol MFM · Paul AK et al. · Aug 1, 2026

This retrospective population-based cohort study of 328,834 singleton pregnancies in Alberta, Canada developed several machine learning models, including gradient-boosted trees and transformer-based architectures, to predict preterm birth at 26 weeks using only administrative health records, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.7704 on a temporally separated holdout set of 67,186 pregnancies from later years. The large scale, chronologically split validation, and use of routinely available data rather than specialized tests make this a credible step toward scalable population-level screening, though the AUC remains moderate and true external validation in another health system is still needed.

Explain this simply

Researchers used AI models to predict which pregnant women were at risk of premature birth by 26 weeks, using only records already collected by the healthcare system, like doctor visits and prescriptions, for over 328,000 pregnancies in Canada. The best model correctly distinguished at-risk from not-at-risk pregnancies about 77% of the time when tested on a separate group of more recent patients, which is a meaningfully large and rigorously tested study, though it has not yet been tried in a different healthcare system.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Gestational Diabetes Prediction 7

Explainable TabNet for Gestational Diabetes Prediction with Physician-In-The-Loop and Multi-Site Clinical Validation.

Int J Med Inform · Narayanan A et al. · Aug 1, 2026

This study developed an explainable TabNet deep learning model for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) prediction using first-antenatal-visit clinical features from 3,525 records, achieving 97.13% accuracy in cross-validation, and then conducted a genuine two-stage physician-in-the-loop (PITL) evaluation plus prospective external validation across three independent Kerala hospitals, where F1-scores dropped to 83.7% to 87.0%, reflecting realistic real-world performance decay. The combination of explainability tools (SHAP, LIME), formal clinician trust assessment, and true multi-site external validation is unusually rigorous for this literature and directly relevant to how such tools might be evaluated before deployment, though the drop from internal to external performance underscores the gap between benchmark and real-world results.

Explain this simply

Researchers built an AI tool to predict gestational diabetes using information collected at a woman's first prenatal visit, and unlike most similar studies, they tested it not just on the original data but also had real doctors review its explanations and then tried it out at three separate hospitals in a different location. The tool worked very well on its original data but performed somewhat worse, though still reasonably, when tested on new hospitals, which is an honest and useful demonstration of how such tools often lose some accuracy in the real world.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Preeclampsia Risk Clustering 7

First-Trimester Machine Learning to Predict Preeclampsia in Normotensive Pregnancies by American Heart Association Guidelines.

Am J Perinatol · Horgan R et al. · Aug 1, 2026

This prospective cohort study across two academic centers applied unsupervised machine learning (k-means clustering) to first-trimester blood pressure data in normotensive pregnancies by American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, identifying a high-risk subgroup with an eight-fold increased adjusted hazard ratio (aHR 8.01) for preeclampsia despite normal blood pressure readings. This is a clinically meaningful finding that could reshape early risk stratification in obstetrics, though it needs external validation before changing practice given its origin from a secondary analysis of two-center data.

Explain this simply

Researchers used an AI clustering method to look at blood pressure patterns in pregnant women who were technically classified as having normal blood pressure, and found a hidden subgroup at eight times higher risk of a dangerous pregnancy complication called preeclampsia. This suggests current blood pressure cutoffs may miss real risk, but the finding needs to be confirmed in more hospitals before it changes practice.

Clinical AI LLM Screening Bias 6

Sociodemographic Bias in Large Language Model Clinical Trial Screening.

J Am Med Inform Assoc · Soffer S et al. · Aug 1, 2026

Across 58 phase II-III randomized controlled trial (RCT) protocols and 5.3 million evaluations, nine large language models (LLMs) showed largely stable eligibility judgments across 33 sociodemographic identity variants, but homelessness produced the largest negative shift in eligibility and related domains like adherence and trust. This is a large-scale, systematic fairness audit relevant to anyone considering LLM-assisted trial screening, showing that bias emerges specifically in inferential judgments about resources and behavior rather than explicit criteria application.

Explain this simply

Scientists tested nine AI language models on nearly 60 real clinical trial protocols, changing only the patient's background details like race, income, or housing status, to see if the AI's decisions about trial eligibility changed unfairly. Race made little difference, but patients labeled as homeless were much more likely to be judged as less eligible or less trustworthy, revealing a specific bias risk if these AI tools were used to screen real patients for trials.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Cervical Cancer Staging 6

Multi-Modal Stacking Machine Learning Model for Predicting Lymph Node Metastasis in Cervical Cancer.

Acad Radiol · Yang H et al. · Jul 29, 2026

This retrospective study with external validation built a stacking ensemble machine learning model combining Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) habitat radiomics with clinical variables to predict lymph node metastasis in 623 early-stage cervical cancer patients, achieving area under the curve (AUC) of 0.875 in the external cohort versus 0.845-0.865 for the best single base model, confirmed by DeLong test and decision curve analysis. The multi-cohort design with genuine external validation is a methodological strength, though the gain over the best base learner is modest and the model still needs prospective testing before it could guide surgical decisions.

Explain this simply

Scientists built an AI system that combines MRI scan patterns with patient information to predict whether cervical cancer has spread to lymph nodes, testing it on over 600 patients including a separate group used only for confirmation. The combined AI model performed slightly better than any single method alone, which is encouraging, but it still needs to be tested in real clinical use before doctors could rely on it to guide surgery decisions.
