Clinical AI AI Genetic Diagnosis 9

AI-Based Clinician Decision Support System for Diagnosis of Inherited Retinal Diseases: A Multicenter, Randomized Trial.

Nat Med · Jia H et al. · Jul 24, 2026

In a multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 295 patients with suspected inherited retinal disease (IRD), an AI-based clinician decision support system (CDSS) called Retina4IRD, built on a Vision Transformer pretrained with RETFound and validated across China, South Korea, and Poland, significantly improved genotype prediction accuracy when used to assist specialists compared to specialists alone (top-5 accuracy 88.5% vs 67.3%, P<0.001), and also improved downstream clinical management scores. This is a rare example of a prospectively validated AI tool with external validation across countries and a hard clinical endpoint (management decisions), making it a strong model for how AI-assisted genetic diagnosis could be integrated into real ophthalmology workflows.

Explain this simply

Scientists built an AI tool that looks at eye scans to help predict which of 17 genetic conditions is causing a patient's inherited eye disease, and tested it in a randomized trial with 295 patients across multiple countries. When doctors used the AI's suggestions, they correctly identified the right genetic cause far more often (89% of the time within their top 5 guesses, versus 67% without the AI) and made better treatment decisions afterward. This is a well-tested, real-world example of AI meaningfully improving diagnosis and patient care, not just a lab experiment.

Clinical AI Speech Biomarkers 8

A Deep Learning Model for Speech-Based Prediction of Clinical Scores in People with Huntington's Disease: A Longitudinal Study with Cross-Sectional Replication.

Lancet Digit Health · Le Moine Veillon C et al. · Jul 23, 2026

A deep learning model (NDSNet) combining a pretrained wav2vec 2.0 speech encoder with a recurrent attentive network was trained on speech recordings from three prospective longitudinal cohorts of Huntington's disease patients and healthy controls, then externally validated in an independent replication cohort, predicting Unified Huntington's Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS) motor scores with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.87, comparable to inter-clinician reliability (ICC 0.847). This is a rare example of a speech biomarker for a neurodegenerative disease with genuine external validation and longitudinal tracking of individual change, suggesting real potential as a low-burden, remote progression marker, though it is Huntington-specific and its generalization to other neurodegenerative diseases is untested.

Explain this simply

Researchers built an AI system that listens to how people with Huntington's disease speak and estimates their clinical disability scores, testing it on recordings from several long-term studies and then checking it again on a completely separate group of patients. The AI's estimates matched actual doctor-assessed scores about as closely as two different doctors would agree with each other, suggesting speech could become an easy, non-invasive way to track this disease over time, though it's only been shown to work for Huntington's so far.

Clinical AI AI Psychosis Screening 6

Evaluating Large Language Models for Assessment of Psychosis Risk.

NPJ Digit Med · Zhu T et al. · Jul 23, 2026

Researchers evaluated 11 open-weight large language models (LLMs) on 678 partial interview transcripts from 373 participants to assess clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR-P), finding the best model (Llama-3.3-70B) achieved 80% accuracy, 93% sensitivity, and 58% specificity against researcher-rated diagnoses, with symptom severity scores correlating well with human raters (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) around 0.74-0.75). The multi-site design and attention to confabulation rates (3%) strengthen the evidence, though the low specificity and reliance on transcript-based assessment limit its readiness for standalone clinical use.

Explain this simply

Scientists tested 11 different AI language models to see if they could read transcripts of psychiatric interviews and correctly flag people at risk for psychosis, comparing the AI's judgments to those of trained clinicians. The best AI model correctly identified 93% of at-risk individuals but also had many false positives, correctly ruling out only 58% of people who were not at risk, and it rarely made up false information (only 3% of summaries). This suggests AI could help scale up early psychosis screening, but it still needs improvement before it can replace expert judgment.

AI in OB/GYN and REI Cervical Screening AI 6

An Explainable Machine Learning Approach for Cervical Cancer Screening: Decoding Morphological Diagnostic Drivers.

Acta Clin Belg · Chang H et al. · Jul 29, 2026

In a retrospective cohort of 5,000 women, this interpretable machine learning (ML) pipeline for liquid-based cytology (LBC) detected high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions or worse (HSIL+) with 92.5% sensitivity versus 84.2% for a double-blind panel of senior cytopathologists, achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94, and showed no performance drop across menopausal or infected subgroups while cutting review time by 63%. The head-to-head comparison against expert pathologists and use of Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for interpretability strengthen the findings, though single-center retrospective design and lack of external validation temper how confidently this generalizes to other populations and lab workflows.

Explain this simply

Researchers built an AI system to read cervical cancer screening slides and tested it against a panel of senior expert pathologists on 5,000 patient samples, finding the AI caught more true cases of serious pre-cancer changes, 92.5% of the time compared to 84.2% for the human experts, while also working just as well in older or infected patients and cutting the time needed to review each slide by nearly two thirds. This is a strong single-site study with a fair head-to-head comparison to real doctors, but it still needs to be tested at other hospitals and with other patient populations before we know it works as well everywhere.

Reply to the Letter to the Editor: "Machine Learning Model for Predicting Preeclampsia-Related Adverse Outcomes".

Pregnancy Hypertens · Hackelöer M et al. · Jul 22, 2026

This is a reply to a letter to the editor addressing methodological critiques of a previously published gradient-boosted tree model that predicts preeclampsia-related adverse outcomes, reporting external validation area under the curve (AUC) of 92% in a German cohort and 87% in a North American cohort, alongside calibration data (Brier scores 0.010 and 0.068). The exchange usefully clarifies the model's incremental value over the soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 to placental growth factor ratio (sFlt-1/PlGF) and its stable discrimination despite cohort heterogeneity, which matters for anyone evaluating this tool's readiness for clinical use, though a formal head-to-head comparison and clinical utility analysis are still pending.

Explain this simply

This is a follow-up discussion where the authors of an earlier study respond to criticism of their AI model that predicts serious complications from preeclampsia, showing that the model performed consistently well when tested on patients from two different countries. This matters because it adds confidence that the tool could work reliably in different settings, although the authors still need to directly compare it against an existing blood test and show it actually helps in real clinical decisions.

Multi-Modal Stacking Machine Learning Model for Predicting Lymph Node Metastasis in Cervical Cancer.

Acad Radiol · Yang H et al. · Jul 29, 2026

This retrospective study with prospective external validation (n=125) built a stacking ensemble model combining tumor and peritumoral radiomic habitats from multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with clinical variables to predict lymph node metastasis (LNM) in early-stage cervical cancer, achieving area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.915, 0.895, and 0.875 across training, internal, and external cohorts, outperforming single classifiers. The multi-cohort design and formal statistical comparison (DeLong test) add credibility, but the model remains imaging-based diagnostics rather than a practice-changing intervention and needs prospective clinical impact testing.

Explain this simply

Researchers built a computer model that combines MRI scan patterns with patient data to predict whether cervical cancer has spread to lymph nodes before surgery, testing it on separate groups of patients including one from a different hospital, and it worked better than simpler versions of itself, correctly telling apart cases about 87 to 92 percent of the time depending on the group. This is a solid technical improvement but still needs to be tested in real clinical use to see if it actually changes how doctors treat patients.

This decision-tree modeling study estimated that AI-assisted digital cytology (Genius) would reduce cervical cytology slide review time from 12.9 to 4.0 minutes per slide in the UK National Health Service (NHS) screening program, translating to a modeled 69% productivity gain across roughly 479,000 slides annually. Non-Contrast MRI Radiomics Nomogram for Predicting Recurrence and Progression-Free Survival in Cervical Cancer Following Concurrent Chemoradiotherapy.

Curr Med Imaging · Wang W et al. · Jul 23, 2026

This two-center retrospective study built an extreme gradient boosting radiomics nomogram from non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to predict recurrence and progression-free survival (PFS) in 145 cervical cancer patients after concurrent chemoradiotherapy, showing significant risk stratification in both the training cohort (n=114, P<0.001) and an external validation cohort (n=31, P=0.012). The use of external validation and a gadolinium-free approach for resource-limited settings strengthens its practical relevance, though the validation cohort is small.

Explain this simply

Researchers built an AI tool that reads regular MRI scans, without needing an injected contrast dye, to predict which cervical cancer patients are likely to have their cancer come back after treatment, and it worked in both the group used to build it and a separate test group from another hospital. This matters because it could help identify high-risk patients using cheaper, safer scans, though the outside test group was quite small.
